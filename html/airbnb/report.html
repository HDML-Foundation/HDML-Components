Ten European cities, 51 707 listings

What the Airbnb data actually says — about price, place, hosts and what a rating is worth. Ten figures, all live. The most important one is that the obvious way to look at this data gives the wrong answer.

1 What you are looking at

One row is one listing offer: a property, in a city, priced for either a weekday or a weekend stay. There is no date anywhere in the schema and no booking record — this is a snapshot of what was advertised, not of what was sold. realsum is the total price for the stay in euros, not a nightly rate, which is why section 3 divides it by guest count before comparing cities.

Consequences worth holding onto: nothing here is a time series; "weekend" is a two-valued category, not a calendar; and a property appearing in both the weekday and weekend table is two rows, not one property observed twice — nothing in the data links them.

2 Amsterdam costs three and a half Athenses

Median stay: €460 in Amsterdam, €128 in Athens. The top decile spreads wider still — €988 against €238.

Two bars per city: the median listing and the 90th percentile. The gap between them is how unequal each city's supply is — Amsterdam, London and Barcelona have long upper tails, while Lisbon, Vienna and Athens are comparatively flat markets where the expensive listings are not that much more expensive.

3 Per guest, the gap is wider — and the order changes

Per person the spread is 5×, not 3.5×: €184 in Amsterdam against €37 in Athens.

Dividing by person_capacity is not cosmetic — it reorders the table. Lisbon has the fifth-highest median stay (€225) but the sixth-lowest price per guest (€68), because what it rents is bigger. Barcelona moves the other way. A ranking of "expensive cities" that never divides by capacity is comparing a studio in one city with an apartment in another.

4 The tail is long enough to break an average

Half of all listings sit between €149 and €320. The 99th percentile is €1 155 and the maximum is €18 545 — 88× the median.

The quantile curve is plotted on a log axis because on a linear one the bottom three quarters of the market collapse into the axis. The practical consequence: never use the mean on this column. The mean stay is €280 against a median of €212, and the gap is entirely the top percentile, which carries 7.6 % of all the money on offer.

5 The weekend premium is not universal

Amsterdam charges +11.0 % for a weekend. Athens charges −5.3 % — weekends are cheaper there, and in Paris too.

This is the dataset's one built-in experiment, and the answer is that "weekends cost more" is a property of some markets and not of the market. The two cities where weekends are cheaper are the two with the largest share of long-stay and business demand in the week; the two with the biggest premium — Amsterdam and Budapest, both near +10 % — are short-break destinations. Six of the ten sit between 0 and +4 %, which is close enough to nothing.

6 Distance does not matter — and that is wrong

Pooled across all ten cities, the median price falls from €229 within one kilometre of the centre to €161 beyond nine. Across ten kilometres, that is almost a flat line — and the correlation is −0.045, which is nothing.

Taken at face value this says location does not price a listing, which contradicts everything known about property. The figure below is the naive answer, and section 7 is why it is wrong.

7 Split by city and the effect reappears

Within every single city, price falls sharply with distance. London: €627 in the first kilometre, €199 beyond five — a 68 % drop. The log-price/distance correlation is negative in all ten cities, from −0.19 to −0.41.

This is Simpson's paradox, and the mechanism is simple once seen: the cheap cities are compact and the expensive ones sprawl. Athens has almost no listings past seven kilometres — its line simply stops, because there is no data there rather than because the price is zero. London and Amsterdam extend far out, and their outer suburbs are still more expensive than central Athens. Pooling the cities lets the between-city price gap swamp the within-city gradient, and the real effect cancels out to nothing.

The same figure carries the pooled line, dashed, for comparison. It is the flattest thing on the chart, and every individual city is steeper than it. Whenever a pooled correlation is suspiciously near zero over data that has an obvious grouping, this is the first thing to check.

8 What actually moves the price

Nothing correlates strongly. The best single predictor is the attraction index at 0.29 — and guest satisfaction is at −0.002, which is to say price and rating are unrelated.

Read the last two bars carefully, because they are the most commercially interesting numbers in the dataset: neither cleanliness (−0.006) nor guest satisfaction (−0.002) has any relationship with what a listing charges. Paying twice as much buys no measurable increase in how much guests liked it. Whatever price is signalling here, it is location and capacity — not quality.

Both distance measures come out near zero for the reason section 7 gives — these are pooled correlations, and the within-city figure is four to eight times stronger. The bar for centre distance is a measurement of the paradox rather than of the city.

9 Superhosts charge less and score higher

Superhost median €199 against €214 for everyone else — and a satisfaction score of 96.9 against 91.2. The badge is not a price premium; it is a different kind of host.

The same split runs through every host attribute. Hosts with several listings charge less (€196 vs €222) and rate slightly better. Business hosts are the mirror image of superhosts: the highest median price on the chart, €228, and the lowest satisfaction, 90.1. Read together, the two figures below say that the professionalised end of this market charges more and pleases less, while the badge that signals quality is attached to hosts who undercharge.

A truncated axis usually deserves suspicion, so: the satisfaction figure starts at 85 because every segment lies between 89.7 and 96.9, and a zero-based axis would render all nine bars visually identical. The differences are small in absolute terms and completely consistent in sign.

10 Cleanliness is the rating

Cleanliness and overall satisfaction correlate at 0.714 — by far the strongest relationship in the data. At cleanliness 10 the mean satisfaction is 96.3; at cleanliness 2 it is 30.1.

The curve is close to a straight line across the whole range, and it is much steeper than anything price does. A host who cannot change their location or their square metres can still move the only score guests read, and this is the lever. The caveat is direction: a guest who disliked a stay may mark everything down together, so part of the 0.714 is a halo rather than causation. The size of it is still unmatched by any other pair of columns.

Weight the left-hand end lightly. Only 143 listings score cleanliness 2 and just 10 score 3, against 30 067 at 10 — the left of the curve is a handful of very bad stays, and the right is most of the market.
