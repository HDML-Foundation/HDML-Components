6 137 titles, and most of them are new

What the Netflix catalog looks like when you ask it seven questions. Every number is computed by Trino at page load from six models; nothing here is hardcoded.

1 What you are looking at

A snapshot of the Netflix catalog: 6 137 titles — 3 831 films and 2 306 series — with 81 355 acting and directing credits against them, and two independent audience scores per title where they exist. The structural reading of the same schema, including the four defects you inherit from its CSV origin, is in the profile page.

One snapshot, no dates attached. There is no "added to Netflix" column and no territory column, so nothing on this page can speak about change over time, or about what is available where. §9 is the full list of what the data cannot support.

2 This is a recent catalog, not a library

85% of the catalog was released in 2015 or later. The four years 2020–2023 hold 2 709 titles — nearly as many as the entire 2010s (2 951), and more than every year before 2010 put together, twelve times over.

Everything below is conditioned on that, which is why it comes first. This is not a sample of cinema; it is a sample of what one streaming service was carrying, weighted overwhelmingly toward its own recent commissioning. The 477 titles released before 2010 are a curated remainder, and they behave differently from the bulk — they are the survivors, so they score higher and carry far more votes than a random title from 2021.

The 2020s bar covers four years, every other bar covers ten. Read it as a rate and the slope is steeper still.

3 Series outscore films, and it is not close

Series average 6.99 on IMDb, films 6.27 — a gap of 0.72, on a scale where the middle 80% of the catalog spans about three points.

Mean and median agree for both types, so this is not one tail dragging an average around. The plausible mechanism is survivor bias in the other direction: a series has to clear a commissioning bar and then survive to air, and the ones that do not are never catalogued as a series at all. A film that disappoints still exists. Among titles with more than 50 000 votes the top two are series and clear of the field — Breaking Bad at 9.5 and Avatar: The Last Airbender at 9.3, above a crowded tie at 9.0 that holds six more series and two films.

4 Most series never get a second season

1 396 of 2 306 series — 61% — have exactly one season. Only 118 reach six or more.

The drop from one season to two is the steepest in the whole distribution: 1 396 to 407, a loss of 71% at the first step. After that the decay is gentle, roughly halving every two seasons. A series that gets renewed once is a substantially different proposition from one that does not, and the data says the decision is made almost entirely at that first gate.

Anthologies and limited series are designed to end after one season and are counted here as if they were cancelled. Nothing in the schema distinguishes them, so the 61% is an upper bound on genuine non-renewal.

5 Two audiences, half agreeing

5 487 titles carry both an IMDb and a TMDB score. If the two platforms were measuring the same thing, the scatter would be a line.

The two scores correlate at r = 0.533. That is a relationship, not an agreement — about 28% of the variance in one is explained by the other.

For two ten-point scales pointed at the same 5 487 works, this is a weak result, and it should change how you use either number. The disagreements are not small: Storybots Laugh, Learn, Sing is 8.7 on IMDb and 2.0 on TMDB. Me Against You: Mr. S's Vendetta runs the other way, 1.6 against 8.1. A 6.5-point gap on a 10-point scale is not noise around a shared signal; it is two different populations voting.

The sensible reading is that neither is "the" score. IMDb skews older, more western and far more heavily voted; TMDB is smaller and more evenly spread across languages. A title's score tells you about the platform's audience at least as much as about the title, and any ranking you build should say which one it used.

6 Genre moves the score by about a point

war (7.14) and history (7.13) top the table; horror (6.02) sits alone at the bottom, a full 1.1 points below the leaders and 0.35 below the next-worst genre.

Documentation is third at 7.02 across 926 titles, which is the most robust of the high scores — the genres above it are small (151 and 275 titles) and could move. The ordering is roughly "genres with an obligation to a real subject" at the top and "genres with an obligation to an effect" at the bottom, with drama, comedy and action bunched indistinguishably in the middle between 6.4 and 6.6.

A title carries 2.57 genres, so these groups overlap heavily and none of this is causal. horror scoring low is partly horror titles being cheap to make, which the schema cannot see.

7 An American catalog with an Indian second act

The United States accounts for 2 325 titles. India is second with 656 — more than the United Kingdom (421) and Japan (304) combined.

That second place is the finding, and it shows up again in the credits: the most-credited actor in the entire catalog is Shah Rukh Khan, with 38 titles, and five of the seven most-credited actors are Indian. Nothing about a naive reading of "a streaming catalog" predicts that, and it is a direct consequence of §2 — this is a recent catalog, and recent commissioning went where the subscriber growth was.

Co-productions count once per country, so the bars sum past 6 137. 176 titles list no country at all and are excluded.

8 Attention is far more unequal than quality

The median title has 2 176 votes. The 99th percentile has 345 907 — 159 times more — and the maximum, The Dark Knight, has 2 684 317, or 1 233× the median.

Note the axis is logarithmic and the bars still climb like that. Scores span a factor of six across the whole catalog; votes span a factor of a thousand. Whatever "popular" means here, it is not a variable with a meaningful average — the mean vote count (21 150) sits above the 80th percentile (14 359), so four titles in five are below average by construction.

And it barely tracks quality: score against log votes correlates at r = 0.223. Being widely seen and being well regarded are close to independent in this catalog, which is the single most useful thing on this page if you are ranking titles. Sort by score and you surface obscure series; sort by votes and you surface old films. Neither is wrong; they are different questions.

9 What this data cannot tell you

Five questions this schema looks like it can answer and cannot:

QuestionWhy not
What is popular on Netflix? There is no viewing data. imdb_votes and tmdb_popularity measure activity on other platforms entirely.
How has the catalog changed? One snapshot, and release_year is when the work was made, not when it was added or removed.
What is available in my country? production_countries is where it was made. There is no availability column.
Is Netflix commissioning more? Nothing marks a Netflix original. §2's growth is catalog growth of every kind mixed together.
Did a series get cancelled? seasons is a count, not an outcome. §4's 61% includes every limited series that ended as designed.
