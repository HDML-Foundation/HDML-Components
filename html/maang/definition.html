The MAANG dataset

Twenty-three years of daily prices for five large-cap technology stocks. This page describes what is in it and what it says — every figure is a live query against the same frame.

1 What it is

Five tables in the maang schema, one per ticker, each a daily OHLCV series. The model joins them full-outer on date, so a day is one row across all five — and the two stocks that listed later leave genuine nulls in the early years rather than zeros.

Every column is stored as TEXT; the frame casts on the way out. The last trading day is 2023-03-17.

One property is worth establishing before any of the figures below, because all of them lean on it: the series is split-adjusted throughout. close and adj_close never diverge by more than a dividend — there is no discontinuity anywhere in their ratio, at any of the splits these five went through. So a fall in share volume is a real fall, not an artifact of a 20-for-1 that reset the denominator.

2 Liquidity collapsed

Every one of the five trades a fraction of the shares it did before 2009 — and the decline is monotone enough to be structural rather than cyclical.

The y axis is logarithmic, and deliberately: on a linear scale Apple's billion-share years flatten Netflix and Microsoft into the axis, and the fact that all five decline together is exactly what a linear chart hides. On a log axis a constant slope is a constant rate, so the five are directly comparable despite spanning three orders of magnitude.

3 Volatility has regimes

The average day got calmer for seventeen years — 6.5 % of the close in 2000 down to 1.5 % in 2017 — and every crisis is a spike off that floor.

The measure is the mean daily high−low range as a percentage of that day's close: a scale-free proxy for realised volatility that needs no returns, and therefore no window function. Read the spikes as dates — 2008, 2018, 2020, 2022 — and the dashed line as the whole-period mean of 2.95 %.

4 They are the same phenomenon

Volume is a fear gauge. Plot the two previous figures against each other and the years line up along a rising diagonal.

Each dot is one ticker-year: its mean daily range on x, its mean daily volume on y. What looked like two independent secular trends is one relationship — quiet years are thin years. Measured on daily data the link is direct: the correlation between volume and the size of that day's move runs from 0.37 (Google) to 0.58 (Amazon).

5 The outcomes are not close

Netflix returned 254× and Microsoft 7.7×. Calling these five a peer group is a claim the data does not support.

Log scale again, and here it is doing real work: on a log axis slope is growth rate, so the five curves can be compared without normalising any of them to a common base — which would have needed a window function this vocabulary cannot express.

The multiples above are measured from each ticker's own first day, and those days differ by four and a half years. Google's 41× excludes the 2000–2004 period; Netflix's 254× starts from a post-IPO price of well under a dollar, split-adjusted. Compare the slopes, not the endpoints.

6 The winners are the ones that fell hardest

Amazon lost 93 % of its value and Netflix 82 % — the same two names that returned 22× and 254×. Holding the winner meant holding through that.

Drawdown is the distance below the highest close reached so far, so it needs a running peak — max() over an unbounded preceding frame — which is why this figure is the first one sourced from this page's own maang_daily model rather than from the tenant's frame. The line for each year is the deepest point reached in it.

Read the troughs as dates: Amazon bottoms in September 2001 at −93.3 %, Apple in April 2003 at −81.8 %, Google in November 2008 at −65.3 %, Microsoft in March 2009 at −68.7 %, and Netflix — the only one whose worst moment belongs to no market-wide crisis — in September 2012 at −82.0 %.

7 Two of them pay you

close ÷ adj_close separates the dividend payers from the non-payers with no other information at all.

Because adj_close is back-adjusted from the present, the ratio at year t is the cumulative dividend adjustment still owed to that year's price — so it decays to 1.0 at the final row by construction, and its height at the left edge is how much dividend income the series has thrown off since. Microsoft enters the window at 1.60 and Apple at 1.17. Amazon, Google and Netflix sit exactly on the dashed 1.0 line for all twenty-three years: none of them paid a dividend inside it. It is the cheapest structural fact in the dataset, and it is legible only because the source carries both an adjusted and an unadjusted close.

8 They have become one trade

The average correlation between any two of these five has roughly tripled — 0.13 in 2013 against 0.70 in 2018 — and has not gone back down.

This is the strongest thing in the dataset and the one that most needs the model layer: it is the mean of all ten pairwise correlations of daily returns, so it needs returns, and returns need lag(). The frame does the easy half — corr() is an ordinary aggregate, ten of them summed and divided by ten in a single hdml-field — while the model does the half a field cannot express.

Read it against figure 3. The two crisis spikes line up, which is the familiar result that diversification fails exactly when it is needed. But the level after 2017 is the finding: correlation is now high in calm years too. Whatever made these five independent businesses in 2013 has stopped being visible in their prices.

Restricted to 2005 onward, because before Google's August 2004 listing only three or six of the ten pairs exist, and a mean over a changing denominator is not a series. The 2000–2004 values — 0.28, 0.43, 0.30, 0.33, 0.13 — are not comparable with what follows and are excluded rather than plotted.

This table is static HTML, and it is the only number on the page that is. It is not a limit of the vocabulary — a second type="query" model returning ten rows (pair, corr) would make it an hdml-bar on an ordinal y — but it would duplicate the returns CTE at a different row grain to render ten numbers that a ranking already reads better as a table. Say the word and it becomes a figure.

Correlating the price levels instead needs no model at all, since corr() is an aggregate — and it would return roughly 0.9 for every pair and mean nothing: five series that all trend upward are correlated by construction. It is left out on purpose.
