MAANG — structural profile

What is in the tenant-a_maang catalog, measured rather than assumed: sizes, keys, cardinalities, value ranges — and the hdml-model those measurements argue for.

1 The catalog

One HDML connection, tenant-a_maang, over a local PostgreSQL. The connection name is the Trino catalog name, so a table is addressed with all three parts — and the hyphen in the tenant prefix means the catalog identifier must be quoted: 

identifier="`tenant-a_maang`.`maang`.`amazon`"

Backticks in an identifier are decoded to double quotes on the way to Trino, which rejects backquoted identifiers outright. Write backticks in HDML; expect "tenant-a_maang"."maang"."amazon" in the SQL.

2 Five tables, and date is a key

Row count equals distinct-date count in every table. date is unique, so a join on it cannot fan out.

That is the single most important fact for modelling this catalog. The tenant's model full-outer-joins all five on date; if any table had repeated dates, that join would multiply rows silently and every aggregate downstream would be wrong. The two bars per table below are the check — they are equal by measurement, not by assumption.

The union of all five date sets is 5 839 days. All five tables cover 4 677 of them; 564 days have four tables and 598 have three.

So a full-outer join yields 5 839 rows, of which only 80 % are complete. Those 1 162 partial rows are why the tenant's frame coalesces five *_date columns into one, and why any cross-ticker measure has to decide what an absent leg means.

3 Coverage over time

Rows per year. The plateau at roughly 252 is the US trading calendar; the two ramps are listing dates, and the short bar at 2023 is the March cut-off rather than missing data.

4 The column dictionary

All seven columns in all five tables are text and nullable — and date is the last column, not the first.

The shape is identical across the five tables, which is what makes one model over all of them reasonable. But nothing is typed at the source, so every numeric operation is a cast, and a model that declares no type hands raw varchar to everything downstream. Section 8 fixes that.

5 Cardinality: what is a key, what is a measure

Distinct values per column. date sits exactly at the row count — the definition of a key. volume is just below it, so it is effectively continuous. The price columns fall further, and Microsoft's fall furthest — 3 682 distinct high values across 5 839 rows — because prices are quoted on a tick grid and a low-volatility stock revisits the same ticks for years.

The axis is linear, not log, and deliberately: every value here sits between 3 682 and 5 839, and a log axis would compress exactly the differences the figure exists to show. A log axis would also break the bars — the simple y form desugars to a baseline of zero, which has no position on a logarithmic scale.

6 Value ranges: how wide each column runs

volume reaches 7.42 × 10⁹ — past the top of a signed 32-bit integer. Declaring it int-32 overflows on real rows.

Each bar spans the observed minimum to the observed maximum, pooled across all five tables, on a log axis — five decades in one figure. The four price columns share a range of roughly 0.23 to 700; volume sits four orders of magnitude above them. This is the figure that sizes the types in section 8.

7 Every cast is safe

The columns are untyped, so the question that decides whether a model can declare types at all is whether the text actually parses. Measured across all 27 435 rows:

CheckViolationsConsequence
NULL or empty string, any column0No coalesce needed at the source
Value not parseable as a number0cast(… as double) cannot fail
date not exactly 10 characters0Uniform ISO; cast(… as date) is safe
volume with a decimal point0Integral — int-64 is exact, not a rounding
volume equal to zero0Safe as a divisor

Prices carry up to 17 decimal places — the residue of back-adjusting for splits, not real precision. That rules out decimal: a scale wide enough to hold them is meaningless, and a narrower one silently rounds. float-64 is the honest type.

8 The model these measurements argue for

Everything above resolves into four decisions — date as a typed key, prices as float-64, volume as int-64, and the join declared on the column proven unique. Per table: 

<hdml-table name="amazon" type="table"
            identifier="`tenant-a_maang`.`maang`.`amazon`">
  <!-- unique, ISO, always 10 chars: a real key -->
  <hdml-field name="date"      type="date"></hdml-field>

  <!-- 0.23 … 700.99, up to 17 decimals: not decimal -->
  <hdml-field name="open"      type="float-64"></hdml-field>
  <hdml-field name="high"      type="float-64"></hdml-field>
  <hdml-field name="low"       type="float-64"></hdml-field>
  <hdml-field name="close"     type="float-64"></hdml-field>
  <hdml-field name="adj_close" type="float-64"></hdml-field>

  <!-- integral, max 7.42e9: int-32 overflows -->
  <hdml-field name="volume"    type="int-64"></hdml-field>
</hdml-table>

And the join, once per pair, on the column section 2 proved unique — a keys filter rather than an expr one, so the planner sees an equijoin: 

<hdml-join type="full-outer" left="amazon" right="apple">
  <hdml-connective operator="and">
    <hdml-filter type="keys" left="date" right="date"></hdml-filter>
  </hdml-connective>
</hdml-join>

Two consequences worth stating before anyone builds on it. Model fields surface downstream as {table}_{field}, so a frame over this model reads amazon_volume and never volume. And full-outer is the right join only if partial rows are wanted: 1 162 of the 5 839 days will carry nulls for at least one ticker. An inner join gives 4 677 complete days instead, and for anything cross-sectional — correlations, index weights, rankings — that is the more honest shape.

What is not measured here: on-disk size. Trino's PostgreSQL connector exposes no relation-size statistic, and pg_relation_size is not reachable through it, so byte counts would have to come from a direct Postgres session rather than from this catalog. Row counts and cardinalities are the size proxies this page can honestly report.
